Unlimit Accounts

Redesigning a B2B bank on a design system: from an audit of the old backend to a phone that signs every payment.

Role
Product Designer
Platforms
Web and mobile app
Year
2025–2026
Company
Unlimit

01 — Before

A powerful bank in an awkward wrapper

A B2B bank is more complex than a retail one. A payment can’t simply be sent: it is created, then it collects two signatures, signature A and signature B, and only then goes out. Behind this sits a model of roles and access rights: who creates, who signs, who can only view. Add multi-currency accounts and cards, and you get a product where every interface mistake costs the client money and time.

The old interface technically handled all of this, but it looked dated and did a poor job of explaining what was going on. Clients couldn’t tell what stage a payment was at or whose signature was needed next, and their questions piled up with support managers. The data confirmed it: the old interface scored 58 out of 100 on SUS against an industry average of 68, and one client in seven contacted support every month. The job wasn’t to refresh the visuals. It was to make complex logic clear, without losing a single scenario on the way, and to prove the result with metrics.

02 — Research

First, understand what already works

I started with an audit, not with screens. I went through the old bank’s backend to see the real functionality: which operations, statuses and roles actually exist, not only the ones the interface shows. In parallel I talked to support managers, who see every day where clients stumble, and to the clients themselves.

The third source was product analytics. I broke down the funnels of the key scenarios, found the steps where people get stuck and matched them with the topics of support requests. Together with NPS, CSAT and SUS surveys, this gave us baseline KPIs, the starting point we later measured the redesign against.

The outcome was a set of functional requirements: a full map of what the bank can do, plus the changes users and support had been waiting for. This document became a shared language for design, engineering and business, and a safeguard against losing a critical scenario in the redesign. I came back to it at every step that followed.

03 — Solution

A design system as the foundation

To keep the redesign from turning into a repaint, I started with the design system: tokens, typography, components and patterns for banking scenarios, such as transaction tables, payment statuses, and amount and currency fields. Light and dark themes are built in at the token level, so every new screen works in both from day one.

The most unusual part was how the mockups came about. I built the design system together with an AI agent, and then the agent assembled every screen on top of it. I deliberately didn’t fix mockups by hand. When a screen came out wrong, I fixed the cause, the requirement or the component, not the symptom. That way every fix improved the whole system, not just one screen.

04 — Impact

Before → after

The new bank closed the pain points found in research, above all in the scenarios that are hardest for B2B. We measured the effect with product metrics, comparing the quarter before launch with the quarter after.

05 — Mobile

The second key to every payment

The mobile app was built in parallel on the same design system, and it has a special role. It works as the second authentication factor: to sign a payment, the user confirms it through a push notification. The balance matters here. Confirming should take seconds, yet before tapping, the person sees whose login it is, what will happen and the exact amount, with a timer running, so they never sign more than they meant to. Signing stopped being a bottleneck, which is one of the reasons CSAT grew.

The same app gives a one-time passcode for signing in on the web, and keeps the security settings in one place: passcode, QR scanner, PIN and biometrics. Beyond that, it holds what is handier in a pocket: accounts and cards, transaction history, notifications. Thanks to the shared component system, web and mobile look and behave like one product.

06 — Result

More than a redesign

The bank got a modern interface where complex B2B scenarios make sense without a call to support, and the team got a single source of truth for design and engineering. NPS rose from +12 to +38, and thanks to the design system new features reach clients three times faster.

Most importantly, the design system outgrew a single product. The onboarding system for new clients and the brand’s crypto products are built on it, so the project set the direction for the whole ecosystem.

  1. 01The web bank and the mobile app on one design system
  2. 02Light and dark themes out of the box
  3. 03A foundation for onboarding and crypto products
  4. 04Mockups assembled by an agent from the system and the requirements, with no drawing by hand

07 — Takeaways

What I took away

The project changed how I see a designer’s role. When an agent assembles the mockups, the value moves to understanding the product, precise requirements and a well-built system: the agent only scales what is already well described.

In B2B, where every screen involves money and access rights, that precision is what separates a convenient product from a risky one. And KPIs fixed before the start turned the conversation about design from “like it or not” into a conversation about results.

Payment Pages

Hosted checkout for a thousand payment methods: a design system, a JSON configurator and a customiser merchants run themselves.

Role
Product Designer
Platforms
Payment pages for web and mobile web, a customiser in the merchant dashboard
Years
2022–2025
Company
Unlimit

01 — Before

Pages nobody had designed

Payment Pages was meant to be a product without a front end: merchants were expected to plug in an SDK and build the interface themselves. So when I joined, the backend generated the pages as bare HTML, with no framework and no design system. Each of the ~80 payment methods had to be drawn, coded and built into the backend separately.

Customisation was even harder. To make a page look like the merchant’s site, a request went through a chain of merchant → support → CSS file → merchant → support → designer → engineering. The designer checked by hand that the styles didn’t break the page, and engineering made a separate build for each client.

Meanwhile the company was entering Latin America and Africa, and a wave of merchants and new methods was on its way. The look of a payment page isn’t cosmetic. It opens in a new tab, and if it doesn’t look like the shop, the buyer starts to doubt. According to Baymard Institute, 19% of users abandon checkout because they don’t trust the site with their card details. The old process would not have survived that scale.

02 — Initiative

Spot the bottleneck before it becomes a crisis

Nobody gave me this task; I came to it myself. I measured how long it took to launch one method and to deliver one customisation, went through the support requests and laid all of it over the plan for new markets. The picture was clear: at the expected growth the team would drown in launches, and the customisation queue would grow faster than anyone could clear it.

I brought two initiatives to the business:

  1. 01
    A design system for payment pages, to cut the time to market of new methods.
  2. 02
    Self-serve customisation in the merchant dashboard, with a limited set of colours and fonts: a merchant can’t break the page, but can make it their own.

03 — Design system

One system for a thousand different methods

The hardest part was the variety of methods. A card form, a PIX QR code in Brazil, an OXXO voucher with a barcode that people pay in cash at a shop, a PagoEfectivo code, a long step-by-step instruction: all of it had to live in one system. Buyers in these markets are used to their own methods, so I studied local patterns and built them into the components instead of inventing new ones.

That is how the design system of universal blocks came about: amount and order details, QR code, barcode, step-by-step instructions, payment deadline, statuses. Together with engineering we built a JSON configurator: a method’s page is assembled from a JSON description, and any change shows on the page at once. Launching a new method turned from coding HTML into describing a configuration.

04 — Customisation

Customisation merchants do themselves

The second part is the customiser in the merchant dashboard. At its heart is a colour algorithm I came up with: the merchant sets one or two colours, the brand colour and the background, and the algorithm picks the other shades for buttons, fields, text and states. Whatever colours they choose, the page looks whole and stays readable.

I deliberately limited the rest: a curated list of fonts and three corner styles, from square through medium to fully rounded pill buttons. These limits are a guarantee. A merchant can’t break the page, so a designer no longer has to check every customisation by hand. The seven-link chain shrank to one: the merchant sets up the page and sees the result straight away.

05 — Impact

We tracked the result with product and business metrics, from launch speed to conversion and merchant loyalty.

06 — Result

Trust that converts

The product grew from ~120 to ~3000 merchants and from ~80 to more than 1000 methods, and neither design nor support became a bottleneck. About 90% of merchants now use customisation: it used to be too complex, and now it takes minutes.

Conversion grew too, and I see two effects behind it. The pages started to look modern even without customisation, so it is clear at once they aren’t a scam. And customised pages look like the merchant’s own site, so the buyer doesn’t lose trust when the new tab opens.

We measured merchant satisfaction through two channels: feedback via support and a survey right inside the customiser. CSAT and NPS rose noticeably: merchants find it convenient, and they stay.

07 — Takeaways

What I took away

It is systems and rules that scale, not screens. I couldn’t draw a thousand methods or check three thousand customisations by hand, so I designed the limits, the algorithm and the configurator that do it for the team.

Strong product decisions start with noticing a bottleneck before anyone else, and coming to the business with numbers and a plan, not a complaint.

Lending Protocol

Crypto loans and deposits for people who aren’t DeFi experts: first the choice of protocol, then risk that anyone can read.

Role
Product Designer, with product analytics and product management
Product
Crypto loans and deposits inside Stable.com
Platforms
Web on desktop and mobile, light and dark themes
Year
2026, from idea to hand-off to engineering

01 — Context

Money that just sits there

Stable.com could do three things: buy stablecoins, sell them and swap one for another. A user moved dollars into crypto, and then the money just sat on the balance. The next step suggested itself: put that money to work. Deposit stablecoins to earn interest, or borrow against crypto without selling it.

That is how the idea of a lending protocol came about. I joined in 2026, when the project had nothing but this idea: no chosen technology, no requirements, no screens. Formally I was the product designer, but at the idea stage design starts long before mockups, so I also had to answer the analyst’s questions and the product manager’s.

The difficulty wasn’t the number of screens but the nature of the product. DeFi lending speaks the language of LTV, health factor and liquidation threshold. A mistake in the interface costs money, not an extra click: if the collateral price falls too far, the protocol automatically sells part of it, and the person loses funds. The task: a user with no DeFi experience should see at a glance how much they will earn, how much they can borrow and at what point they risk losing their collateral.

02 — Protocol

One protocol or two

The first big question wasn’t about the interface but about the foundation: which protocol to build on. The shortlist had three candidates, and it came down to two market leaders, Aave v3 and Morpho. And to a tempting idea from the business side: why not take both and show the user the best rate?

I took both protocols apart not as a developer but as a designer: what picture of the world does each one impose on the user? It turned out that under the same words, deposit, collateral and liquidation, live two different logics.

Aave v3Morpho
StructureOne shared pool: all of an account’s collateral works togetherIsolated markets: one collateral → loan pair with its own oracle and threshold
Borrower riskOne health factor for the whole accountEach position has its own liquidation threshold (LLTV)
LiquidationWhen the health factor drops below 1; usually up to 50% of the debt at onceWhen LTV reaches LLTV; the position can be closed in full
Risk parametersChanged by governance votesFixed when the market is created
DepositorPuts an asset into the pool and receives aTokensPuts it into a vault; a curator spreads the money across markets

Sources: Aave v3 documentation, Morpho vs Aave

Combining both would make the user hold two risk models in their head. In one card the safety margin is a single number for the whole account; in the next, one number per loan. For a product whose main job is to explain risk, that is the worst case. For the team it meant a double integration, double risk monitoring and twice as many edge cases.

I proposed staying on Morpho and not combining protocols, for four reasons:

  1. 01
    A clear mental model. An isolated market is “one loan, one collateral”, as at a bank. A drop in one asset doesn’t touch the other positions.
  2. 02
    A simple deposit. A vault gives the depositor a “put it in and earn” scenario, and a professional curator spreads the money across markets.
  3. 03
    Stable terms. Market parameters won’t change by a vote halfway through a loan, so they are easier to explain and to promise.
  4. 04
    Proven in mass products. Coinbase, Ledger and Crypto.com already build loans and deposits on Morpho; more on that in the next section.

The business accepted the arguments. Instead of “the best rate on the market” we chose understandable risk, and that decision shaped all the design that followed.

03 — Research

How others packaged it

To keep the protocol recommendation from staying theory, I looked at how companies with the same audience as Stable.com, people with crypto but not DeFi experts, built loans and deposits into their apps. Separately I studied the pro interfaces of Aave and Morpho themselves as an anti-reference: every protocol number is on screen at once, and a newcomer drowns in them.

ProductProtocolWhat it offersWhat I took for Stable.com
CoinbaseMorpho, Base networkUSDC loans against BTC, ETH and other assets; USDC deposits through a curator’s vaultsThe protocol is invisible: “borrow USDC against BTC”, not “the cbBTC/USDC market”. No repayment schedule: repay whenever it suits you
Ledger WalletMorphoAn Earn section: yield on USDC and USDTBuy, swap and earn interest in one app: exactly the Stable.com user’s path
Crypto.comMorpho, Cronos networkYield on stablecoins and loans against ETH and BTCLending lives inside the main interface, with no separate wallet or platform
Aave AppAaveA stablecoin savings account you can top up by cardThe “savings account” metaphor for deposits: earnings instead of APY jargon

Three conclusions came out of it, and they later became design principles:

  1. 01
    Sell the task, not the protocol. None of the mass products make you pick a market or a vault; the user chooses “earn” or “borrow”.
  2. 02
    Lending continues the main scenario. Buy, swap, earn interest: one chain in one product.
  3. 03
    Risk is explained worst of all. Coinbase’s loans page describes it in one sentence in percentages: LTV must stay below 86%, or the collateral is sold (source). For someone without DeFi experience that is an abstraction. This is where I saw a chance to do better than the market.

04 — Use cases

From an idea to a map of scenarios

Next I gathered the business requirements. Not only the “what”, loans and deposits, but also the “how”: lending had to feel like part of Stable.com, not a DeFi terminal bolted on. The same language, the same visual style, the same logic as buying, selling and swapping.

The requirements grew into use cases. I described each one as a scenario with preconditions, steps, errors and edge cases, so that design, development and testing could all work from them at once. The edge cases aren’t a formality here: the loan rate floats, the collateral price can move between the quote and the confirmation, and a deposit can only be withdrawn within the available liquidity.

Laying out the scenarios, I noticed the main thing: for a borrower, the most important scenario is not getting a loan but living with it. Watching the safety margin and reacting in time when the collateral price falls. That loop became the centre of the design.

When the collateral price falls, the user gets a warning and brings the loan back into the safe zone. Without a reaction the protocol liquidates part of the collateral, and that is exactly the outcome the design has to prevent.

05 — Drafts

Show the risk without scaring people

In the drafts I pitted two opposite concepts against each other. The first was a “pro dashboard” in the spirit of the protocols’ own interfaces: every parameter of the position on one screen. The second was a “financial product”: the deposit looks like a savings account, the loan like a bank loan, and protocol details open on request. The second won. It continued the language of Stable.com and answered the main finding of the research: risk has to be explained, not just shown.

Six key decisions grew out of this concept:

  1. 01
    The language of the task, not of the protocol. Buttons say Earn and Borrow; the protocol’s Supply never appears. Words like Morpho, vault and LLTV live in a “How it works” block, not in the main flow.
  2. 02
    Risk in money, not in ratios. Instead of a health factor, a “margin to liquidation” scale with three zones and the collateral price at which liquidation starts: “If BTC drops to $61,400, part of your collateral will be sold.”
  3. 03
    A safe default. The loan calculator starts at an amount in the green zone, not at the maximum. To borrow more, the user deliberately moves the slider and watches the margin shrink.
  4. 04
    Everything visible before confirming. As soon as an amount is entered, the rate, the monthly cost of the loan and the liquidation price are calculated. The confirmation screen repeats it all in one summary.
  5. 05
    An honest floating rate. The rate is labelled “now”, with its recent history next to it. Nobody should find out after the fact that the rate went up.
  6. 06
    A warning before liquidation. When a loan moves into the yellow or red zone, a notification arrives with Add collateral and Repay part buttons. Fixing it takes one tap.

06 — Build

A design system and AI agents

Lending didn’t start from a blank page. I already had the Stable.com design system, on which I had built buying, selling and swapping. I only added what was missing: the safety margin scale, the loan card, the loan calculator, the floating rate block. The new components worked in light and dark themes, on desktop and mobile, from the start.

The mockups were assembled by AI agents. The use cases lived in Confluence as a table with a separate UI column. An agent took a scenario and assembled the flow in Figma from the system’s ready components only, in four variants at once: desktop and mobile, light and dark. Then it checked the flow against the scenario and wrote the link to the screens back into the UI column.

This created an end-to-end link: every requirement has a screen, and every screen has a requirement. When a screen came out wrong, I fixed the cause, the wording of the use case or the component, not the mockup, and rebuilt. Every fix improved the system, not one screen.

What went to engineering wasn’t a set of pictures but a single package: use cases, requirements and screens, linked to each other. A developer doesn’t have to guess which rule a screen belongs to; the answer is in the same table row.

07 — Metrics

How we’ll know it works

I set the metrics before hand-off, so that after launch we discuss the result in numbers, not impressions. Each one tests a specific design decision, and the main one is the liquidation rate: if people lose collateral, the interface has failed at its main job.

08 — Result

From one sentence to a product in development

Lending went from an idea to a package for engineering, and at every step I was responsible for the decision, not just the screens.

  1. 01
    The stack is chosen: Morpho, without mixing protocols. The team avoided a double integration, and users avoided two risk models
  2. 02
    The competitor review turned into three design principles
  3. 03
    Depositor and borrower use cases are described together with their edge cases
  4. 04
    Every scenario is assembled in four variants and linked to its requirements
  5. 05
    The Stable.com design system gained components for financial risk that the next products will use
  6. 06
    KPIs are fixed before launch

09 — Takeaways

What I took away

Design starts before screens. Choosing a protocol looked like a technical task, but it was the first UX decision: the protocol sets how the user will think about their risk.

Had I joined at the mockup stage, we would have been drawing an interface on top of two incompatible logics.

Second, in a financial product clarity matters more than yield. The best rate sells the first deposit; understandable risk sells the second and the tenth.

Third, agents only scale what is well described. The more precise the use case and the better thought-out the system, the less manual work, and the closer the mockups are to what ships. The designer’s role shifts from drawing to decisions, and that is where it brings the most value.
